# MiCA

The **Markets in Crypto-Assets Regulation**, known as **MiCA** or **MiCAR**, is
Regulation (EU) 2023/1114, the European Union's framework for crypto-assets
that existing financial services law does not already cover. It sets rules for
offers of crypto-assets to the public, for issuers of
[stablecoins](stablecoin.md) (called asset-referenced tokens and e-money
tokens), for crypto-asset service providers such as exchanges and custodians,
and for market abuse. A provider authorised in one member state may serve
clients across the EU. The stablecoin rules applied from 30 June 2024 and the
remainder from 30 December 2024, and the last national grandfathering periods
for service providers ended on 1 July 2026.[^reg][^esma-eot] MiCA shaped the
euro and dollar stablecoin market in Europe, led Coinbase Europe to delist
[Tether](tether.md)'s USDT, and is the subject of an open dispute over whether
the same stablecoin may be issued both inside and outside the
EU.[^decrypt-coinbase][^skadden]

## Background

Before MiCA, EU law covered crypto-assets only when they qualified as
financial instruments, deposits, funds or similar categories. National regimes
filled the gap unevenly. France, for example, had a regime created by its PACTE
law in 2019 for token offerings and digital asset service
providers.[^amf-deal] On 24 September 2020 the European Commission adopted a
digital finance package that included a proposal for a regulation on markets in
crypto-assets. The Commission described it as a bespoke regime for crypto-assets
not covered by existing legislation, including "stablecoins", with
authorisation requirements for issuers and service providers and tighter
requirements for issuers of significant asset-backed tokens.[^ec-package]

## Legislative history

The Council presidency and the European Parliament reached a provisional
agreement on 30 June 2022 at the end of trilogue negotiations. France's
financial markets authority, the AMF, reported that the deal would apply 18
months after entry into force and that NFTs would generally be excluded, with
the Commission to assess within 18 months whether a specific NFT regime was
needed.[^amf-deal]

Parliament approved MiCA on 20 April 2023, by 517 votes to 38 with 18
abstentions, in the same session as a separate law extending the "travel rule"
for transfers of crypto-assets.[^ep-vote] The Council adopted the Regulation on
16 May 2023, and it was signed on 31 May 2023 and published in the Official
Journal on 9 June 2023.[^reg] It entered into force on the twentieth day after
publication.[^reg]

Under Article 149, Titles III and IV, on asset-referenced tokens and e-money
tokens, apply from 30 June 2024. The rest of the Regulation applies from 30
December 2024. Certain provisions that empower the Commission, the EBA and ESMA
to adopt technical standards applied from 29 June 2023.[^reg]

## Provisions

### Scope and exclusions

MiCA applies to people who issue or offer crypto-assets to the public, seek
their admission to trading, or provide services relating to them in the
Union.[^reg] It does not apply to crypto-assets that are financial
instruments, deposits, funds (unless they are e-money tokens), securitisation
positions, insurance and pension products and similar categories listed in
Article 2(4).[^reg] ESMA published guidelines in December 2024 on when a
crypto-asset qualifies as a financial instrument.[^esma-dec24]

Two exclusions matter for the market. First, Article 2(3) excludes
crypto-assets that are unique and not fungible with other crypto-assets. The
preamble says issuing tokens as NFTs in a large series or collection should be
treated as an indicator of fungibility, and that attributing a unique
identifier to a token is not by itself enough to make it non-fungible.[^reg]
Second, the preamble says that where crypto-asset services are provided "in a
fully decentralised manner without any intermediary", they fall outside the
Regulation. The same recital says the Regulation covers activities that are
partly performed in a decentralised manner, and that crypto-assets with no
identifiable issuer fall outside Titles II to IV, although service providers
dealing in them are covered.[^reg] Article 142 required the Commission to
report by 30 December 2024 on decentralised finance, crypto lending and
borrowing, and the treatment of unique and non-fungible crypto-assets, with a
legislative proposal where appropriate.[^reg]

### Other crypto-assets

Crypto-assets that are neither asset-referenced tokens nor e-money tokens, such
as most tokens traded on exchanges, fall under Title II. An offeror must be a
legal person and must draw up, notify and publish a crypto-asset white paper.
Competent authorities do not approve the white paper before
publication.[^reg]

### Asset-referenced tokens

Article 3 defines an asset-referenced token as a crypto-asset that is not an
e-money token and that purports to maintain a stable value by referencing
another value or right or a combination of them, including one or more
official currencies.[^reg]

An issuer must be authorised by its home member state, or be a credit
institution that follows a white-paper procedure. Issuers with an average
outstanding value that never exceeds €5 million over 12 months, and offers
limited to qualified investors, are exempt from authorisation but must still
publish a white paper.[^reg] The authorisation is valid across the Union.[^reg]

Issuers must hold own funds equal to the highest of €350,000, 2% of the
average reserve of assets, and a quarter of the previous year's fixed
overheads.[^reg] They must constitute and maintain a reserve of assets that is
legally and operationally segregated from the issuer's estate and managed to
cover the risks of the referenced assets and the liquidity risk of redemption.
Reserve assets must not be encumbered, must be held in custody under
conditions the Regulation sets, and may be invested only in highly liquid
financial instruments with minimal market, credit and concentration
risk.[^reg] Holders have a right of redemption against the issuer at all
times, either in funds equal to the market value of the referenced assets or
by delivery of those assets.[^reg] Issuers and service providers may not grant
interest, and any remuneration or benefit tied to the length of time a holder
keeps the token counts as interest.[^reg]

If an asset-referenced token is widely used as a means of exchange within a
single currency area, above 1 million transactions and €200 million a day, the
issuer must stop issuing it and submit a plan to bring use below those
levels.[^reg]

### E-money tokens

An e-money token is a crypto-asset that purports to maintain a stable value by
referencing the value of one official currency. E-money tokens are deemed to be
electronic money.[^reg] Only a credit institution or an electronic money
institution may offer one to the public, and only after notifying a white
paper to its competent authority, at least 40 working days before the intended
offer.[^reg]

Issuers must issue the token at par on receipt of funds. On request, they must
redeem it at any time at par in funds (not in electronic money), and holders
have a claim on the issuer. Redemption is not subject to a fee, without prejudice to
Article 46.[^reg] Issuers and service providers may not grant
interest, defined as for asset-referenced tokens.[^reg] At least 30% of the
funds received must be held in separate accounts at credit institutions, and
the rest invested in secure, low-risk assets denominated in the referenced
currency.[^reg]

### Significant tokens and the EBA

A token is classified as significant by the EBA when at least three of the
criteria in Article 43 are met. The listed criteria include more than 10
million holders, a value, market capitalisation or reserve above €5 billion,
more than 2.5 million transactions and €500 million in transactions a day, and
the interconnectedness of the token or its issuer with the financial system.
The same criteria apply to e-money tokens.[^reg]

Issuers of significant asset-referenced tokens come under EBA supervision, as
do issuers of significant e-money tokens that are electronic money institutions
with respect to the specific additional obligations in Articles 55 and 58. The
EBA chairs a consultative supervisory college for each such issuer. Significant
issuers face extra obligations, including liquidity stress testing and
liquidity requirements for which the EBA is to develop technical standards,
including a minimum amount of deposits in each referenced currency that cannot
be lower than 60% of the amount referenced.[^reg]

### Crypto-asset service providers

Article 59 bars the provision of crypto-asset services in the Union unless the
provider is a legal person authorised under Article 63 or is a financial entity
such as a credit institution, investment firm or electronic money institution
that is allowed to provide such services under Article 60. Authorised providers
need a registered office in a member state where they carry out part of their
services, effective management in the Union, and at least one director resident
in the Union.[^reg]

A provider that wants to serve clients in other member states notifies its home
authority, which informs the host states, ESMA and the EBA within 10 working
days. The provider may start in another member state on receipt of that
communication, or at the latest 15 calendar days after submitting the
information.[^reg] This is the passport.

A provider is significant if it has at least 15 million active users in the
Union on average over a calendar year. The home authority then reports annually
to ESMA's Board of Supervisors.[^reg]

### Transitional period

Article 143 allowed providers that operated lawfully under national law before
30 December 2024 to continue until 1 July 2026 or until they were granted or
refused authorisation, whichever came first. Member states could opt out of
the regime or shorten it where their previous national rules were less strict,
and could apply a simplified procedure to applications from entities already
authorised under national law that were submitted between 30 December 2024 and
1 July 2026.[^reg] Issuers of asset-referenced tokens that were not credit
institutions and were already issuing before 30 June 2024 could continue only
if they applied for authorisation before 30 July 2024.[^reg]

### Market abuse

Title VI applies to acts concerning crypto-assets admitted to trading or for
which admission has been requested, whether on or off a trading platform, and
to acts in third countries.[^reg] It requires issuers, offerors and persons
seeking admission to disclose inside information, prohibits insider dealing
and unlawful disclosure, and prohibits market manipulation. People who
professionally arrange or execute transactions in crypto-assets must have
systems to prevent and detect market abuse and report suspicious orders.[^reg]

## Implementation

ESMA said in December 2024 that it had delivered more than 30 technical
standards and guidelines over 18 months, many with the EBA. Its last package
that month covered technical standards on detecting market abuse, and
guidelines on reverse solicitation, suitability, transfer services, the
qualification of crypto-assets as financial instruments and ICT security.
The reverse solicitation guidelines say the exemption is narrow and applies
only where the client is the exclusive initiator of the service.[^esma-dec24]

On 17 April 2026 ESMA published a statement on the end of the transitional
period. It said that after 1 July 2026 any entity providing crypto-asset
services to EU clients without a MiCA licence would be in breach of EU law and
must stop. It expected unauthorised providers to have implemented wind-down
plans by that date, authorised providers to onboard existing clients, and
national authorities to act against unauthorised provision. It also said that
non-EU entities may not, outside the narrow reverse solicitation exception,
serve or solicit EU clients, including on a business-to-business basis through
outsourced custody. It told investors to check ESMA's interim MiCA
register.[^esma-eot] A Luxembourg law firm reported that ESMA repeated the
orderly wind-down expectation in a further statement on 23 June
2026.[^elvinger]

ESMA published a peer review in July 2025 of the Malta Financial Services
Authority's authorisation of one crypto-asset service provider, which it did
not name. It found significant issues that were still unresolved when the
authorisation was granted and some risk areas that were not adequately
assessed. It asked all national authorities that authorise providers to pay
attention to business growth, conflicts of interest, governance, ICT
architecture and decentralised products.[^esma-malta]

### Review and centralised supervision

On 20 May 2026 the Commission opened a consultation on reviewing MiCA, with
comments due by 31 August 2026. Topics include the boundary with MiFID II,
multi-issuance, euro stablecoins, the interest ban, lending and borrowing,
staking and decentralised finance.[^skadden] The EBA replied on 24 September
2026, saying that MiCA's requirements for issuers are broadly appropriate but
that third-country multi-issuer schemes pose "significant to very significant
risks", and recommending that the Commission consider regulating crypto lending
and review reserve and reporting requirements.[^eba] As of October 2026 this
article has not confirmed any legislative proposal from the Commission
following the review.

Separately, on 4 December 2025 the Commission published a market integration
and supervision package. ESMA welcomed its proposal to transfer direct
supervision of certain significant cross-border infrastructures and
crypto-asset service providers to the EU level.[^esma-mip] Ledger Insights
reported that the Commission proposed direct ESMA supervision of all
MiCA-regulated service providers.[^ledger-council] On 9 October 2026 the
Council agreed the main elements of its negotiating position. Ledger Insights
reported that it limits ESMA supervision to significant providers, keeps the
15 million user threshold and adds alternative criteria, so that a provider
with just over 1 million users could qualify if it meets other tests such as
assets in custody or trading volume. Commissioner Maria Luís Albuquerque said
the Commission "deeply regrets the proposed compromise".[^ledger-council]
Finance Magnates reported that firms under direct supervision would move to
ESMA over two years.[^fm-council] Parliament has yet to set its position, and
negotiations with the Council can begin only once it does.[^ledger-council]

## Effects

### Tether and USDT

On 13 December 2024 Coinbase Europe and Coinbase Germany delisted six
stablecoins for retail users, including USDT, citing MiCA. Coinbase said USDC
and EURC remained supported because they were MiCA-compliant.[^decrypt-coinbase]
Tether's chief executive Paolo Ardoino said in August 2024 that MiCA creates
"an incredibly big systemic risk", and on 2 July 2026 Crypto Briefing reported
that he confirmed Tether had deliberately not sought a MiCA licence for USDT,
calling the reserve rules "dangerous" and "ill-conceived".[^cointelegraph-ardoino][^cryptobriefing]

Tether announced on 27 November 2024 that it would end support for EURT, its
euro-pegged token, and told holders to redeem by 27 November 2025. It cited
changes in EU stablecoin regulation and falling interest, and said it would
focus on MiCA-compliant euro and dollar tokens from Quantoz Payments.[^block-eurt]

### Circle

On 1 July 2024 Circle announced that its French entity, Circle Internet
Financial Europe SAS, held an electronic money institution licence from
France's prudential supervisor, the ACPR, and that it would issue USDC and
EURC in the EU under MiCA's e-money token rules. Circle called itself the
first global stablecoin issuer to comply, said USDC was the only top-10
stablecoin that was compliant at the time, and said its position was subject to
possible Commission clarifications of the law.[^circle][^decrypt-circle]

### Euro stablecoins and Qivalis

Qivalis is an Amsterdam-based company backed by 37 European banks that plans to
issue a euro stablecoin. On 14 October 2026 it announced that the Dutch central
bank, De Nederlandsche Bank, had authorised it as an electronic money
institution. Issuance remained subject to MiCA's requirements and publication
of a white paper, and Qivalis said it planned to begin within months.[^qivalis-dnb]

### The multi-issuance dispute

In a multi-issuance scheme, an EU entity and a non-EU entity issue fungible
versions of the same token. Reuters
described USDC as the largest EU-regulated multi-issuance stablecoin.[^reuters-multi]

On 3 September 2025 ECB President Christine Lagarde said "gaps remain" in MiCA.
She argued that joint issuance by EU and non-EU entities lets the non-EU part
avoid MiCA's requirements and that in a run on the token, EU-held reserves
"may not be sufficient" for concentrated redemption demand. She called for
legislation barring such schemes unless other jurisdictions have equivalent
regimes and safeguards exist for transfers of assets.[^block-lagarde] On 20
October 2025 the European Systemic Risk Board, which Lagarde chairs, published
a recommendation that the Commission treat third-country multi-issuer schemes
as not permitted under MiCA, to be clarified by the end of 2025. Failing that,
it asked authorities to put safeguards in place, most by the end of
2026.[^esrb]

The Commission has taken a different view. Reuters reported in November 2025
that it saw no need for major changes. The EBA said it was waiting for the
Commission to clarify whether multi-issuance is allowed, that the ESRB's
concerns "reflect the inherent risks" of large redemptions, and that existing
safeguards should be applied.[^reuters-multi] In its May 2026 consultation, the
Commission acknowledged that multi-issuance is currently not prohibited by
MiCA for all stablecoins, and asked whether this should continue, listing
options such as limiting direct redemption to clients of EU-authorised service
providers.[^skadden] Parliament adopted a non-binding own-initiative report on
digital assets in July 2026, by 390 votes to 86, that Ledger Insights said
backs multi-issuance with safeguards.[^ledger-parliament] The EBA's September
2026 response then called for changes to address the risks of third-country
multi-issuer schemes.[^eba]

## Criticism

### Uneven national supervision

On 15 September 2025 the French AMF, Austria's FMA and Italy's Consob
published joint proposals. They said the first months of MiCA had shown "major
differences in how crypto-markets are being supervised by national authorities"
despite ESMA's coordination. They proposed direct ESMA supervision of major
service providers to end opportunistic choice of the authorising country,
stricter rules for non-EU platforms that target European investors, an
independent cybersecurity audit before authorisation, and scrutiny of white
paper filings.[^amf-joint] ESMA's Malta review, two months earlier, raised related
concerns about authorisation (see Implementation).[^esma-malta] The Council's
October 2026 position narrowed the Commission's proposal to centralise
supervision, and Ledger Insights reported the Commission's regret.[^ledger-council]

### ECB and the Commission

The ECB has pressed for tighter rules on foreign stablecoins (see above).
Politico reported in April 2025 that the Commission and ECB clashed over
stablecoin risk, and Ledger Insights said Peter Kerstens of the Commission saw
no need for a "MiCA v2".[^ledger-clash]

### Industry

Tether's chief executive objects to the reserve rules, under which at least 30%
of the funds behind an e-money token, and a minimum of 60% for significant
tokens, are held as bank deposits.[^reg] Ardoino argued the Regulation creates
systemic risk rather than reducing it. In 2026 he gave concentration risk in European banks as his
reason for not seeking a licence.[^cointelegraph-ardoino][^cryptobriefing]
Circle's July 2024 release described its compliance as subject to
clarification by the Commission.[^circle]
