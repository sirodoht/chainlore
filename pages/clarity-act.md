# CLARITY Act

The **Digital Asset Market Clarity Act**, or **CLARITY Act**, is a bill in
the United States Congress, H.R. 3633 of the 119th Congress, that would set
up a federal framework for the trading of crypto assets other than payment
[stablecoins](stablecoin.md). It is not law. The House of Representatives
passed it on 17 July 2025, but the Senate has not: on 15 September 2026 a
vote to begin debate failed by 49 to 50, short of the 60 votes
needed.[^roll199][^fintechweekly] The bill would divide authority over
crypto markets between the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) and the
Commodity Futures Trading Commission (CFTC), exempt many
decentralised-finance activities from registration, and, in the Senate
Banking text, ban payments on stablecoin balances that work like bank
interest. As of October 2026, the sources opened for this article report no
further floor vote.[^orrick][^fintechweekly]

## Background

Before the bill, no federal statute said which crypto assets are securities
and which are commodities. The House had tried to answer that in the
previous Congress. It passed the Financial Innovation and Technology for
the 21st Century Act (FIT21), H.R. 4763, on 22 May 2024 by 279 to 136, with
71 Democrats voting yes.[^roll226] EY describes FIT21 as a similar bill to
the CLARITY Act.[^ey]

Stablecoins were handled in a separate bill, the [GENIUS
Act](genius-act.md). Because House Financial Services Republicans accepted
the Senate's stablecoin bill unchanged, the House-passed CLARITY Act
included provisions that would reopen that law, on nonfinancial issuers,
commodity-backed stablecoins and issuer accounting.[^ey]

## Legislative history

### House

Rep. French Hill of Arkansas, who chairs the House Financial Services
Committee, introduced H.R. 3633 on 29 May 2025. The original cosponsors
included Glenn Thompson of Pennsylvania, who chairs the Agriculture
Committee, and Reps. Craig, Emmer, Johnson of South Dakota, Davis of North
Carolina, Steil, Torres and Davidson. The bill was referred to both
committees.[^hr-ih][^marketsmedia]

Both committees marked it up on 10 June 2025. Agriculture approved it 47 to
6. Financial Services, whose markup ran longer, advanced it 32 to
19.[^decrypt-committee][^marketsmedia] Democrats offered amendments to bar
the president and his family from profiting from crypto ventures and to
block taxpayer bailouts of token issuers; both were voted down in the
Financial Services markup, according to Decrypt. Hill said at the markup
that "It's not an ethics bill".[^decrypt-committee]

On the floor, conservative Republicans blocked the rule for debate on 15
and 16 July 2025, in a vote held open for nine hours. Leaders then agreed
to attach a separate bill barring a central bank digital currency to the
annual defence authorisation bill.[^ey] The House passed H.R. 3633 on 17
July 2025 by 294 to 134. All 216 Republicans who voted supported it, as did
78 Democrats; 134 Democrats opposed it.[^roll199] The House-passed text
includes a title on central bank digital currency, the Anti-CBDC
Surveillance State Act.[^hr-eh] Maxine Waters, the ranking Democrat on
Financial Services, called the bill the "CALAMITY Act".[^ey]

The bill reached the Senate on 18 September 2025 and was referred to the
Banking Committee.[^hr-rs]

### Senate committees

The Senate split the subject between two committees. The Agriculture
Committee, which has jurisdiction over the CFTC, advanced its own bill, the
Digital Commodity Intermediaries Act, 12 to 11 along party lines on 29
January 2026. Chair John Boozman called it the first step. Democrats
objected that it lacked limits on federal officials issuing or endorsing
digital assets and safeguards against fraud and crime.[^aba-ag] The
Congressional Research Service (CRS) lists it as S. 3755, reported on 2
February 2026.[^crs]

The Banking Committee, which has jurisdiction over the SEC, stalled over
stablecoin interest. On 1 May 2026 draft text emerged from talks between
Senators Thom Tillis (R-NC) and Angela Alsobrooks (D-MD), which the White
House helped facilitate. It banned stablecoin yield tied solely to holding
but allowed rewards tied to bona fide activity. Coinbase's chief legal
officer, Paul Grewal, said the language "preserves activity-based rewards
tied to real participation on crypto platforms and
networks".[^coindesk-yield]

The Banking Committee advanced the bill 15 to 9 on 14 May 2026. All 13
Republicans and two Democrats, Ruben Gallego and Angela Alsobrooks, voted
yes, and nine Democrats voted no. More than 100 amendments were proposed. A
Van Hollen amendment on Trump family conflicts of interest failed, with all
13 Republicans opposed.[^cointelegraph-banking] The committee's reporting
date was 1 June 2026, when Senator Tim Scott reported H.R. 3633 with an
amendment that struck the House text and substituted a different
one.[^hr-rs]

### Senate floor

The Agriculture and Banking texts had to be combined before floor action.
Negotiations over an ethics provision became the main obstacle. On 22 July
2026, seven Senate Democrats said the Republican proposal "falls short".
Alsobrooks said she "absolutely will not support" floor legislation without
ethics provisions.[^cointelegraph-ethics] Republicans posted a revised
substitute on 10 September 2026 that added CFTC registration and Bank
Secrecy Act obligations for trading protocols that are not
decentralised.[^defiant-sept10] Sen. Cynthia Lummis published combined text
on 14 September, merging the Banking and Agriculture texts.[^crs]

The Senate voted on cloture on the motion to proceed to H.R. 3633 on 15
September 2026. Cloture failed 49 to 50; it needed 60. All 49 votes in
favour came from Republicans, and Republicans Susan Collins, Josh Hawley,
Jerry Moran and Tillis voted no. The Defiant reported that Tillis voted no
so that he could enter a motion to reconsider, which he did about a minute
after the result.[^defiant-vote] Because the vote concerned only the motion
to proceed, the Senate never voted on the substance of the
bill.[^fintechweekly]

Two days later, seven Democratic senators who had worked on the text,
including Gallego, Gillibrand and Warner, called the result a setback "but
not the end" and recommitted to a bipartisan deal, according to FinTech
Weekly.[^fintechweekly] The Senate's work period ran from 5 October to 6
November, overlapping the 3 November midterm elections, and FinTech Weekly
called a post-election lame-duck session the most plausible window for
another attempt.[^fintechweekly] As of October 2026, none of the sources
opened reports that a new vote has been scheduled, and the outcome of
Tillis's motion is not reported.[^orrick][^fintechweekly]

## Provisions

The House and Senate texts differ in structure and vocabulary. Provisions
below are labelled by the version they come from: the House-passed text of
17 July 2025 (the engrossed bill), and the Banking Committee substitute
reported in the Senate on 1 June 2026. The Agriculture Committee's bill and
the September combined drafts are described only where a source opened for
this article describes them.

### Division of authority between the SEC and CFTC

*House-passed text.* The House bill defines a "digital commodity" as a
digital asset intrinsically linked to a blockchain system and whose value
is derived, or reasonably expected to be derived, from the use of that
system. It excludes securities, including notes and investment contracts
that give the holder a claim on the revenues, profits or assets of the
issuer.[^hr-eh] The CFTC would have exclusive jurisdiction over spot-market
transactions in digital commodities carried out on, or subject to the rules
of, a registered entity or by a person registered with the CFTC. That
jurisdiction would not cover bank custody activities or the offer and sale
of an investment contract involving a digital commodity.[^hr-eh] Permitted
payment stablecoins, defined by reference to the GENIUS Act, are removed
from the CFTC's general commodity jurisdiction.[^hr-eh]

The House bill treats an investment contract involving a digital commodity
as separate from the digital commodity itself. It amends the securities
laws so that "investment contract" does not include an "investment contract
asset", and exempts certain primary sales by an issuer from registration.
To use the exemption, the blockchain must be certified as mature or be
intended to become mature within four years, the issuer may take in no more
than US$50 million over 12 months (adjusted for inflation), and no
purchaser may hold more than 10 per cent of the outstanding units.[^hr-eh]
A further section would treat digital commodities as "covered securities",
which preempts state securities registration while preserving state
antifraud authority.[^hr-eh]

*Banking Committee substitute.* The Senate Banking text builds the SEC's
role around a different category, the "ancillary asset": a network token
whose value depends on the entrepreneurial or managerial efforts of an
originator or related person. Network tokens are treated as non-securities
for the purposes of the securities laws. The SEC would write a "Regulation
Crypto" with an exemption from registration for offerings of ancillary
assets of up to the greater of US$50 million per calendar year for up to
four years or 10 per cent of the outstanding supply, and an originator
could raise no more than US$200 million in total under the exemption.
Antifraud provisions would continue to apply to those offerings.[^hr-rs]
CRS describes the September combined draft as giving the CFTC exclusive
jurisdiction over cash or spot sales of digital commodities, including
network tokens and ancillary assets outside the SEC's jurisdiction, with
the SEC keeping some authority over primary-market sales of ancillary
assets.[^crs]

### Mature blockchains

*House-passed text.* A "mature blockchain system" is a blockchain system,
together with its digital commodity, that is not controlled by any person
or group of persons under common control. An issuer, a related person, a
decentralised governance system, a registered exchange or another
designated person may certify maturity to the SEC. The certification takes
effect 60 days after filing unless the SEC rebuts it or stays it once for
up to 120 days. The SEC may object within that period.[^hr-eh] Consumer
Reports criticised this process as largely self-attested.[^consumerreports]

*Banking Committee substitute.* The substitute does not use the "mature
blockchain" label. Instead it defines "coordinated control" through SEC
rulemaking, and a network token that depends on such control would be an
ancillary asset.[^hr-rs][^crs]

### Registration of exchanges, brokers and dealers

*House-passed text.* A trading facility that offers a spot market in at
least one digital commodity would register with the CFTC as a "digital
commodity exchange". Exemptions apply for facilities with only a de minimis
amount of trading or that serve customers in a single state.[^hr-eh] The
CFTC would have 180 days after enactment to adopt an expedited registration
process for exchanges, brokers and dealers. Registrants would operate in
provisional status until 270 days after the relevant final rules take
effect.[^hr-eh] Brokers and dealers would register with the CFTC. The bill
also requires the CFTC to write conflict-of-interest rules within 360
days.[^hr-eh]

*Combined September draft.* CRS says exchanges, brokers and dealers would
register with the CFTC, and would become Bank Secrecy Act financial
institutions with anti-money laundering programmes. Exchanges would follow
core principles, customer assets would generally belong to customers and
not be commingled, and the bankruptcy provisions would be amended to cover
holders of digital and ancillary assets.[^crs]

### Decentralised finance and developers

*House-passed text.* Two parallel sections, one in the Securities Exchange
Act and one in the Commodity Exchange Act, provide that a person is not
subject to those Acts on the basis of listed activities in relation to a
blockchain system or decentralised finance trading protocol. The activities
include validating and relaying transactions, running a node or oracle,
providing a read-only user interface, developing or distributing a
blockchain system, participating in a liquidity pool for spot trades, and
writing software that lets users hold their own keys. The SEC's and CFTC's
antifraud and anti-manipulation authority remains.[^hr-eh] Another section
provides that a non-controlling blockchain developer or service provider is
not a money transmitter, and does not face a similar new registration
requirement, solely for publishing software, supplying self-custody tools
or maintaining infrastructure.[^hr-eh]

*Banking Committee substitute.* The substitute includes similar protection
for those who compile or validate transactions, run nodes or oracles, and
develop distributed ledger software, and defines a "decentralized finance
trading protocol" as one that runs on pre-set rules without reliance on a
person for custody, with exclusions where a person or group can materially
alter its operation or censor its use.[^hr-rs] A section titled the
Blockchain Regulatory Certainty Act provides that a "non-controlling"
developer or provider is not a money transmitting business and is not
engaged in money transmitting under 18 U.S.C. 1960. It preserves that
statute's application to a person who acts with specific intent to transfer
funds known to derive from crime or to support unlawful activity.[^hr-rs] A
further title calls for a rulemaking on applying existing securities
intermediary and Bank Secrecy Act requirements to trading protocols that
are not decentralised.[^hr-rs] The 10 September substitute extended CFTC
registration and Bank Secrecy Act duties to non-decentralised protocols and
limited the DeFi title to spot and cash digital commodity
transactions.[^defiant-sept10]

### Stablecoin rewards

*House-passed text.* The House bill refers to the GENIUS Act's definitions
for permitted payment stablecoins but, as read for this article, contains
no counterpart to the Senate's section on interest and yield.[^hr-eh]

*Banking Committee substitute.* Section 404 prohibits any "covered party",
meaning a digital asset service provider and its affiliates, other than a
registered stablecoin issuer, from paying interest or yield to a US
customer or user in two cases: solely for holding payment stablecoins, or
on a stablecoin balance in a way that is economically or functionally
equivalent to interest on a bank deposit. Rewards tied to "bona fide
activities or bona fide transactions" are permitted if they are not
equivalent to deposit interest. The SEC, the CFTC and the Treasury
Secretary must jointly issue rules within one year clarifying the line.
They must include a non-exhaustive list of permitted rewards, such as
rebates for payments, providing liquidity or posting collateral, and
participating in governance or staking, which may be calculated by
reference to a balance, duration or tenure. The section also bars evasion,
protects a firm that relied in good faith on the rules from penalties if it
cures a breach within 90 days, and bars marketing a stablecoin as a deposit
or as government-backed.[^hr-rs] A sense-of-Congress clause states both
that deposit-like payments may inhibit depository institutions and that
activity-based rewards are critical to innovation.[^hr-rs]

CRS notes that the GENIUS Act bars payment stablecoin issuers from paying
interest but does not reach third parties such as exchanges, which banks
call a loophole. The September combined draft would bar third-party yield
equivalent to deposit interest, allow payments tied to bona fide
transactions or activity, and let the Treasury Secretary block third-party
yield within 18 months if it harms community bank deposits.[^crs]
CoinDesk's 1 May report said the earlier text assigned rulemaking to
Treasury and the CFTC; the reported Banking text names the SEC, CFTC and
Treasury jointly.[^coindesk-yield][^hr-rs] The [GENIUS Act](genius-act.md)
restricts issuers and left the exchange question open.[^crs]

### Ethics and other provisions

*House-passed text.* A rule of construction states that existing ethics
laws continue to apply and that they bar members of Congress and senior
executive branch officials from issuing a digital commodity while in public
service.[^hr-eh] The House text also contains the Anti-CBDC Surveillance
State Act, which would bar Federal Reserve banks from offering products
directly to individuals or issuing a central bank digital currency.[^hr-eh]

*Combined September draft.* CRS describes a provision that would bar the
president, vice president, members of Congress, executive branch employees
and their spouses from issuing or sponsoring digital assets for
consideration. Exchanges could not list assets tied to an official who
violates the ban. The Attorney General would enforce it, and state
attorneys general could seek injunctive relief in some cases.[^crs]

## Criticism

### Bank trade groups on stablecoin yield

On 13 July 2026 the American Bankers Association, the Independent Community
Bankers of America and 76 state associations wrote to Senate leaders asking
for changes to section 404 before final passage. They argued that
ambiguities could encourage stablecoin arrangements that function as
substitutes for deposits, and objected to rewards tied to how much and how
long customers hold stablecoins. They asked the Senate to replace the
"functional and economic equivalent" standard with "substantially
similar".[^aba-july] The Consumer Federation of America's Corey Frayer
warned in May that the rulemaking wording could let firms run activities
and pass returns on to customers.[^coindesk-yield] Crypto firms said the
compromise preserved rewards for real participation; Barnali Biswal of
Hilbert Group said bank groups lobbied against the yield language until the
vote.[^coindesk-yield][^coindesk-clarity]

### Ethics and the president's crypto interests

Democrats made an ethics provision a condition for their votes. Elizabeth
Warren said in July that the draft "does nothing to stop President Trump"
from earning more crypto income. Alsobrooks said the Department of Justice
could not be relied on fully for enforcement. Sen. Bernie Moreno called the
ethics language "the most powerful ethics language in US history", and
commentators aligned with the White House argued that the Department of
Justice, not state attorneys general, should enforce federal
law.[^cointelegraph-ethics] Earlier, Democrats had tried and failed to add
conflict-of-interest limits in the House markup and the Senate Banking
markup.[^decrypt-committee][^cointelegraph-banking]

### DeFi, developers and law enforcement

Rep. Sam Liccardo, described by Decrypt as supportive of crypto, questioned
the House bill's DeFi exemption, saying projects might use the label to
avoid regulation.[^decrypt-committee] In May 2026 the National Sheriffs
Association wrote to the Senate Banking Committee that there is no good
reason to give mixers, tumblers and DeFi a blanket exemption, and that
while some software developers are not money transmitters, "plenty of
others are", according to PYMNTS. The report said the White House planned
to speak to law enforcement groups about their objections.[^pymnts] Connor
Howe of Enso told CoinDesk that the September draft dropped explicit
protection under 18 U.S.C. 1960 for developers who never touch customer
funds.[^coindesk-clarity]

### Consumer advocates

Consumer Reports said on the House vote that the bill "prioritizes
regulatory certainty for the crypto industry at the expense of consumer
protection". It objected that most digital assets would move to the CFTC,
which it said lacks a consumer protection mandate, that state consumer laws
would be preempted, that "decentralization" criteria could be exploited,
and that mature status is largely self-certified.[^consumerreports]

### Industry reaction to the failed vote

Ripple's chief executive Brad Garlinghouse said the failure "stings" and
blamed Democratic politics. Others expected the SEC and CFTC to act without
legislation; CoinDesk reported that the SEC had put "Regulation Crypto
Assets" out for comment in August and that CFTC Chair Selig had reportedly
told staff to draft a market-structure regime under existing authority.
Industry figures noted that agency rules can be reversed by a later
administration while a statute cannot.[^coindesk-clarity]
